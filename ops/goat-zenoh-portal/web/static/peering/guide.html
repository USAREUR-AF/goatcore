Peering Guide

You're here because you want to send or receive data on this Zenoh trial fabric and you've never used Zenoh before, or you've used it but never federated two routers. This page is a first-time-friendly walkthrough. Twenty minutes from "I have an empty machine" to "I'm publishing data the rest of the fabric can see."

Everything you need to configure your side lives at Our Config — that page is your reference; this page is the recipe.

1. What Zenoh is, in 90 seconds

Zenoh is a publish-subscribe message fabric. Three concepts cover almost everything:

  • Key expression — the address of a piece of data, like a slash-separated path: acme/sensors/lidar/front/v1. Subscribers match by pattern (acme/** matches everything under acme/).
  • Publisher / Subscriber — your code writes (session.put(key, bytes)) or watches (session.declare_subscriber(key_expr, callback)). Bytes flow asynchronously through the fabric.
  • Router — a Zenoh process that ferries data between publishers and subscribers that can't reach each other directly. On this trial there is one router; your processes connect to it.

That's it. There's no broker concept, no exchange concept, no consumer-group concept — Zenoh is much smaller than Kafka or MQTT in surface area. If you're coming from those tools, the closest mental model is "MQTT, but key-paths are routable patterns and there's no broker process."

Wire format is your choice. Zenoh transports opaque bytes. The fabric doesn't care if your payload is JSON, protobuf, CBOR, MessagePack, or raw bytes. We recommend protobuf with a schema uploaded to the portal (raw .proto source or a pre-compiled .fds; the portal handles either) so the schema viewer + inspector decode work — see the Register a topic page after you're connected.

2. The two ways to join

Both flavors give you the same end result — bytes flowing under your namespace — through the same cert-mint flow under the hood. The difference is whether you bring one process or your own infrastructure.

Flavor A — Publisher

"I'll run one process. Wire it to your router."

Smallest footprint. You install the eclipse-zenoh client library (Python, Rust, Go, etc.) in a single process, point it at our router with mTLS, and publish. The router does the routing.

Flavor B — Router-peer

"I'll run my own zenohd. Federate with yours."

Heavier setup. You run a zenohd process on your side; your local stack (publishers + subscribers + storage) connects to YOUR router. Your router then peers with ours over mTLS, and traffic crosses the boundary.

Pick which?
If…Pick
You have one publisher process and no plans for moreFlavor A
You're sketching / hacking / showing one demoFlavor A
You already run a Zenoh deployment internallyFlavor B
You'll have 3+ publishers / subscribers behind a stable networkFlavor B
You want your downstream consumers to keep working if our router blipsFlavor B
You're one __LEX_ORG__ and the trial is short-livedFlavor A (probably)

3. Flavor A walkthrough — Publisher

The full path from zero to publishing. Pre-reqs: a machine that can reach zenoh.goat.example on TCP 7447 (you must be on the goat NetBird mesh).

3.1 Get an operator to mint you one __LEX_ORG__ row

Hand the operator: your desired prefix (lowercase, no slashes — e.g. my-__LEX_ORG__), a display name, and your contact. They run the existing POST /api/vendors flow and hand you back a bundle tarball + a one-time API key.

Before you even ask: pick a prefix that isn't already taken. The live list is on Our Config under Currently-taken __LEX_ORG__ prefixes, or programmatically: 

curl -s https://portal.goat.example/api/fabric/config | jq '.namespacing'

3.2 Unpack the bundle

The bundle is a tarball; unpack it somewhere safe. You'll see:

my-__LEX_ORG__-bundle/
  <__LEX_ORG__>-cert.pem            # your client cert (signed by us; URI-SAN-pinned)
  <__LEX_ORG__>-key.pem             # your private key (DO NOT share)
  <goatnet>-ca-root.pem   # our root CA (your trust anchor for our router)
  first-publisher.py           # a worked publisher pre-wired to your prefix
  first-subscriber.py          # companion subscriber for self-validation
  zenoh-trial-onboarding.md    # the full onboarding doc (offline copy)
  NOTES.md                     # quick-start cheat sheet
The bundle URL is one-time. If the download fails or you lose the tarball, you need a fresh mint — the URL won't work twice. Capture it carefully.

3.3 Install eclipse-zenoh

pip install eclipse-zenoh protobuf

That's it. eclipse-zenoh is the official upstream client; pinned to 1.9.0 to match the router.

3.4 Run the worked example

cd my-__LEX_ORG__-bundle
python3 first-publisher.py

The script publishes one message per second under your prefix. The whole-block TLS config is already pre-wired (cert paths, mTLS enabled, verify_name_on_connect: true, our endpoint).

3.5 Confirm it's flowing

Open Inspector in the portal, search for your prefix, and you should see your messages arriving in real time. Open Panoscope ↗ and your peer shows up as a new node on the topology graph.

3.5.1 Self-validate with the companion subscriber

The bundle ships a sibling first-subscriber.py alongside the publisher so you can prove the full round-trip in two shells without leaving your laptop. Same cert, same mTLS config — the only difference is declare_subscriber instead of declare_publisher:

# shell A — subscribe to your whole subtree
python3 first-subscriber.py
# subscribed: <__LEX_ORG__>/**
# waiting for samples (Ctrl-C to exit)

# shell B — publish five hello messages
python3 first-publisher.py

Within a second of shell B's first PUT, shell A should print a matching HH:MM:SS <__LEX_ORG__>/hello/v1 text(...): '...' line. If you see them, your cert authenticates correctly AND the router accepts your writes AND your subscription receives them — full round-trip. Pass a key expression as the first arg to narrow: python3 first-subscriber.py "<__LEX_ORG__>/sensors/**" or python3 first-subscriber.py "**" (everything on the fabric — reads are wide-open).

If shell A stays silent after shell B publishes, walk down the Connectivity Probe: DNS → TCP → TLS → cert chain → URI-SAN matches.

3.6 Register your topics + upload protobuf schemas

Recommended — this is what lights up the portal's schema viewer + per-language autogen code + inspector decode for your topics. Two pieces: register the topic (so the portal knows it exists), then upload a schema body (so the portal can decode your wire bytes). The schema upload accepts two shapes:

  • (easy) Raw .proto source. Single file with google/protobuf/* imports only. The portal compiles it server-side — you don't need protoc installed locally. Use this for most cases.
  • (power) Pre-compiled FileDescriptorSet (.fds). The output of protoc --descriptor_set_out=... --include_imports. Use this when your .proto imports other .proto files of yours (multi-file source) — the trial portal's v1.1 server-side compile is single-file only.
key_pattern is a literal string, not a template. Every angle-bracket placeholder you see in the examples below (<__LEX_ORG__>, <system>, <arbitrary>, <MsgName>) is a fill-in-the-blank convention. The portal stores key_pattern byte-for-byte and matches it against your incoming publishes by exact-string equality — no wildcards, no template substitution. Substitute every <...> with a real value here AND in your publisher code; the two strings must be identical.

If your publisher emits acme/radar1/RADAR-EXAMPLE/position_attitude and you register acme/radar1/<system>/position_attitude, those are two different strings — /schema.html will say "topic not found" even while your bytes flow live on the wire.

The portal API key is in your bundle (delivered out-of-band). Set up your shell — substitute the bold parts:

export PORTAL_TOKEN=<your-portal-api-key>     # ← from your OOB bundle
export PORTAL=https://portal.goat.example
export __LEX_ORG_UPPER__=<your-__LEX_ORG__-prefix>          # ← your real prefix, e.g. acme

Step 1 — register the topic. Worked example for __LEX_ORG__ acme publishing on acme/radar1/RADAR-EXAMPLE/position_attitude:

# Publisher code (Python):
pub = session.declare_publisher("acme/radar1/RADAR-EXAMPLE/position_attitude")
                              # ^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^
                              # This EXACT string is what you register below ↓

# Registration call:
curl -sS -X POST "$PORTAL/api/vendors/$__LEX_ORG_UPPER__/topics" \
  -H "Authorization: Bearer $PORTAL_TOKEN" \
  -H "Content-Type: application/json" \
  -d '{
    "key_pattern": "acme/radar1/RADAR-EXAMPLE/position_attitude",
    "format":      "protobuf:acme.radar.v1.PositionAttitude",
    "description": "one-line summary of what this topic carries"
  }'
# {"id": 42, "key_pattern": "acme/radar1/RADAR-EXAMPLE/position_attitude", ...}

The bold parts are what you adapt for your topic. The response carries the new topic's id — you need it for step 2. After the POST, open Register a topic and verify the new row's key_pattern column equals what your publisher actually emits, byte-for-byte.

Step 2a — upload .proto source (easy path, no protoc needed). Use the topic id from step 1:

curl -sS -X POST "$PORTAL/api/vendors/$__LEX_ORG_UPPER__/topics/$TOPIC_ID/schemas" \
  -H "Authorization: Bearer $PORTAL_TOKEN" \
  -F "file=@your_message.proto" \
  -F "format=protobuf-source" \
  -F "version=v1" \
  -F "compatibility=none"

The portal compiles your .proto in-process and persists the resulting FileDescriptorSet. Syntax errors come back as HTTP 400 with a your_message.proto:<line>:<col>: <msg> diagnostic — same shape as protoc locally. Imports limited to google/protobuf/* well-known types.

Step 2b — upload pre-compiled .fds (power path, multi-file source). Required when your .proto imports other .proto files of your own:

protoc \
  --descriptor_set_out=topic.fds \
  --include_imports \
  --include_source_info \
  -I . your_message.proto

curl -sS -X POST "$PORTAL/api/vendors/$__LEX_ORG_UPPER__/topics/$TOPIC_ID/schemas" \
  -H "Authorization: Bearer $PORTAL_TOKEN" \
  -F "file=@topic.fds" \
  -F "format=protobuf-fds" \
  -F "version=v1" \
  -F "compatibility=none"

Once both pieces land, /schema.html?key=<your-real-topic-key> (e.g. ?key=acme/radar1/RADAR-EXAMPLE/position_attitude) shows your messages + fields + reconstructed .proto source + per-language code examples. The Inspector's decode tab will render samples against your descriptor instead of showing raw bytes.

Other valid format values: json, cbor, msgpack, text, raw. Only protobuf:<MsgName> unlocks the schema viewer + decode surfaces. To ship a new schema version without losing v1, re-POST with version=v2 — the viewer's version picker lets consumers walk back.

If /schema.html says "topic not found", step 1 didn't land OR the registered key doesn't match what your publisher actually emits. The most common cause is the literal-string-vs-template mistake called out at the top of this section — open Register a topic and verify the key_pattern column equals the key URL you're querying. If they differ, edit the row (or DELETE + re-POST) to match what your publisher emits — don't try to make the publisher emit a templated string.

If /schema.html shows the topic but says no schema is uploaded, step 2 didn't land — check the upload curl returned 200.

4. Flavor B walkthrough — Router-peer

Flavor B has the same cert-mint flow as Flavor A — you still ask the operator for one __LEX_ORG__ row; you still get a bundle. The difference: instead of running first-publisher.py directly, you put the cert + key into your own zenohd process, and your stack talks to your router.

4.1 Mint your __LEX_ORG__ row (same as Flavor A 3.1)

Same flow; identical bundle output. The cert + key + root-CA stay the same.

4.2 Install zenohd on your side

One option: run the upstream container image, version-pinned to match ours:

docker pull eclipse/zenoh:1.9.0
# (or use a digest pin: eclipse/zenoh:1.9.0@sha256:<...>)

Other options: build from source (github.com/eclipse-zenoh/zenoh), grab a release binary, or use your distro's package. Match version 1.9.0 exactly — cross-version peering between different majors is not guaranteed.

4.3 Write your zenohd.json5

Easiest path: open the Router Config Generator, punch in your intended prefix, click Generate, then copy or download. Or curl it: 

curl -s "https://portal.goat.example/api/fabric/zenohd-config?prefix=my-__LEX_ORG__" \
  > zenohd.json5

If you'd rather hand-roll, here's the same template — replace <your-prefix> conceptually (the prefix doesn't appear in the file; see callout below) and the cert paths: 

{
  // Your router connects OUT to ours; downstream clients connect to localhost.
  mode: "router",
  connect: {
    endpoints: ["tls/zenoh.goat.example:7447"],
  },
  listen: {
    endpoints: ["tcp/127.0.0.1:7447"],   // your downstream clients connect here
  },
  transport: {
    link: {
      tls: {
        enable_mtls: true,
        verify_name_on_connect: true,
        root_ca_certificate: "/path/to/root-cert.pem",
        listen_private_key: "/path/to/key.pem",
        listen_certificate: "/path/to/cert.pem",
        connect_private_key: "/path/to/key.pem",
        connect_certificate: "/path/to/cert.pem",
      },
    },
  },
  access_control: {
    // Your downstream stack is local + trusted; the trust boundary is the
    // mTLS upstream to us. We enforce ACL on our side; you can run open
    // locally if you want.
    enabled: false,
  },
}
Your prefix doesn't appear in zenohd.json5 directly — our router enforces "you can only publish under <your-prefix>/**" via the cert's URI-SAN. Trying to publish under another prefix gets dropped at our router, with the rejection logged.

4.4 Start zenohd

docker run --rm -d --name my-zenoh \
  -v "$(pwd)/zenohd.json5:/zenoh.json5:ro" \
  -v "$(pwd)/cert.pem:/cert.pem:ro" \
  -v "$(pwd)/key.pem:/key.pem:ro" \
  -v "$(pwd)/root-cert.pem:/root-cert.pem:ro" \
  -p 7447:7447 \
  eclipse/zenoh:1.9.0 -c /zenoh.json5

(Adjust the cert paths inside zenohd.json5 to /cert.pem / /key.pem / /root-cert.pem to match the bind-mounts.)

4.5 Point your downstream stack at tcp/127.0.0.1:7447

Your publishers + subscribers all connect to YOUR router. They don't need certs — the trust boundary is your router's upstream mTLS to us.

4.6 Verify federation

Publish something from your downstream side; check Inspector on our portal. You should see the bytes within a second or two. Open Panoscope ↗ and your router shows up as a peer on the topology graph.

5. What's deliberately NOT in this trial

This is a short, time-boxed trial fabric. Many things you'd expect from a production federation are out of scope:

  • No approval ladder. An operator mints one __LEX_ORG__ row directly. The production substrate has a multi-step approval workflow + offline-CA YubiKey-PIV ceremony (ADR 0856); the trial collapses that to one operator action.
  • No release/<partner>/... namespace. Production reserves a separate trust-tier prefix per ADR 0821. The trial uses the same <__LEX_ORG__>/<system>/<arbitrary>/v<N> namespace for everyone — partners and __LEX_ORGS__ are structurally identical.
  • No reciprocity. If you want us to publish data to you as well, that's a separate operator conversation. The trial's wire-level ACL only matches your URI-SAN against your own prefix.
  • No heterogeneous pub/sub. Zenoh-to-Zenoh only. If your existing stack is Kafka / MQTT / NATS / DDS / ROS2 and you want a protocol bridge, that's deferred to a goat-translator follow-on.
  • No 24/7 ops. Expect best-effort operator availability, not on-call.

6. Getting help / reporting bugs

Stuck? Bring the operator the following and they can help in <5 minutes:

  1. Your __LEX_ORG__ prefix
  2. The exact error message from your zenoh client (or the zenohd log if you're on Flavor B)
  3. The wire-format you're publishing as (JSON / protobuf / etc.)
  4. Whether you can reach zenoh.goat.example:7447 from your machine
